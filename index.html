Findy

Less overwhelm. More independence.
An iOS accessibility concept that helps seniors navigate the unfamiliar with confidence.

01 / Problem Discovery

What makes an unfamiliar phone task difficult?

Findy began with a visit to a senior center in Huntington Beach. We wanted to understand where older adults got stuck with unfamiliar technology and how they could navigate it more independently.

Starting point

We started by observing everyday technology use.

At a senior center in Huntington Beach and Adrianna Senior Home, we noticed moments of confusion and hesitation during technology use. These observations gave us a starting point, but they did not explain what was making the experience difficult.

Before choosing a solution, we needed to learn more about unfamiliar interfaces, uncertainty about the next action, and how people preferred to receive help.

What we wanted to understand

  1. 01

    Where do people get stuck when an interface is unfamiliar?

  2. 02

    What makes someone hesitate before taking the next step?

  3. 03

    What kind of help lets people continue the task themselves?

How Can We...

Help seniors overcome the overwhelm of everyday technology

While keeping them empowered and in control

So technology becomes a tool, not a barrier

02 / User Research

Looking beyond the survey answers

Survey responses suggested that frustration was uncommon. Our observations raised further questions, so we followed up with in-person research to understand where difficulties arose.

The initial survey

When asked how often they felt frustrated or overwhelmed with their device, 73.3% of the 15 respondents selected “Rarely.”

We had also observed moments of confusion in person. The survey alone could not explain when those moments happened or what made them difficult. We needed to look at the experience more closely.

A closer look

We followed up with in-person conversations.

We conducted eight interviews across two senior centers and held a Socratic seminar. These conversations helped us explore differences in technology experience, sources of uncertainty, and expectations of support.

What we learned

01 / Individual differences

People need different kinds of support.

Some participants already used AI, while others struggled to find apps on the home screen. This range made it clear that we could not design around a single level of familiarity with technology.

Design implication: Let people choose how they communicate with Findy.

02 / Overwhelm

An unclear next step can interrupt a task.

Paula felt overwhelmed by visual clutter and worried about doing something wrong. Her experience shifted our attention to the moment before a tap: how could the interface make the next action easier to understand?

Design implication: Direct attention to one action at a time.

03 / Independence

Getting help should still leave room to learn.

When family members took over her phone to solve a problem, Joanne did not learn the steps herself. Getting the task done left her without a process she could repeat. This pointed us toward guidance that keeps the user involved.

Expert perspective: Susan Turk Charles emphasized individual differences and the importance of helping people learn without taking over.

Design implication: Offer guidance while keeping the user in control of each step.

The design goal

How might we make the next step clearer without taking control?

This became the focus of Findy: guidance that supports the user through a task without taking it over.

Our solution

Meet Findy

Built into iOS, Findy is an AI guide that lives on a senior’s existing iPhone and walks alongside them through the unfamiliar, so they learn, build confidence, and stay independent.

01 / Understanding the workflow

One system, two different responsibilities.

Before designing screens, our team mapped the journeys of a call center manager and a staff member. Looking at their actions, pain points, and moments of uncertainty helped us distinguish setting the plan from keeping the plan up to date.

John · Call center manager

Sets provider quotas across clinic locations, manages provider information, and needs an overview of daily progress to coordinate the team.

Sarah · Call center staff

Updates appointment progress throughout the day and needs changes to be visible to colleagues without coordinating across separate tools.

Existing tools missed the healthcare workflow.

We compared Connecteam, Paradime, Salesforce, and Assembled. Their workforce features were useful, but they did not fit CLCHC’s combination of daily quotas per provider, a specific permission hierarchy, and progress visibility across clinics. The opportunity was to build around how this team actually works.

02 / Testing and iteration

Small changes made the workflow easier to follow.

Wireframes became interactive prototypes, which we tested with John in two usability sessions. His feedback changed how actions were organized and how editing worked as the directory grew.

  1. 01

    Bring related actions together.

    Separate add and manage buttons became one unified action, reducing visual clutter and making the next step easier to find.

  2. 02

    Give tag editing a dedicated space.

    We moved tag creation from an inline interaction into a sidebar, giving users more control while keeping the main workflow in view.

  3. 03

    Design editing for a growing directory.

    Inline editing initially seemed familiar. Testing revealed that a wider provider table forced users to scroll sideways to reach fields. We chose a consistent sidebar editor so updates remained easy to access as the system expanded.

In the follow-up session, John navigated with more confidence and completed the editing flow without additional explanation.

Quota Management System

Managers set daily quotas by provider, location, and working hours. Staff update completion with simple plus and minus controls, while shared progress updates keep the team aligned. A consistent sidebar lets managers create or adjust quotas without leaving the overview.

A shared overview of daily progress, completion rates, and quotas needing attention.

Review provider, hours, location, and quota details before confirming.

Provider Management Directory

Managers maintain provider information in one directory, reuse tags such as languages, and add or reorder categories as the center’s needs change. The sidebar editing pattern keeps updates accessible without navigating sideways through a growing table.

Searchable provider records with reusable tags and flexible categories.

Create and review provider details in a consistent sidebar.

03 / Supporting everyday use

Access that reflects the team’s responsibilities.

The workflow extends beyond the two dashboards. Sign-in, user permissions, and settings help the center manage who can access the system and what each person can change.

Familiar sign-in, clear approval.

Staff sign in with their existing Google accounts. New users request approval before accessing the platform, and managers can update team roles through the user directory.

Controls matched to each role.

Staff update quota progress; managers create and edit quotas. Settings support profile updates, quota calculation factors, and a confirmation step when transferring the master role.
